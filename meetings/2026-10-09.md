## AI and theoretical computer science

We discussed both the opportunities and the risks of using AI in theoretical computer science (TCS). AI can help us learn and make progress in research, but we must retain responsibility for our own thinking, understanding, and the work we share.

### Opportunities for learning and research

Many areas of TCS have a steep learning curve. AI can help students work through unfamiliar material and mathematical tools more quickly, making it easier to learn what they need and begin applying it to research. Used thoughtfully, it can help researchers achieve better results faster.

### Independent thinking and research credit

We need to be conscious of the risk of letting AI replace our own thinking and understanding. Guiding an AI through many conversations toward a research goal can create a false sense of ownership of the result. That process alone does not justify taking credit for research whose technical details we do not understand and whose correctness we cannot check. We should be able to explain the reasoning ourselves and evaluate whether it is sound.

### Our no-AI-slop policy

Our team does not allow sharing AI-generated research material that we have not understood and checked. Fear of being scooped does not justify rushing an unchecked, AI-generated paper into public view.

It is fine to keep AI-generated ideas and drafts privately while taking the time to digest them. Before sharing or publishing the resulting work, we must understand the techniques, check correctness, and present and write it based on our own understanding. Producing a polished-looking document is not a substitute for doing that work.

## Why take notes

Taking notes records your own thoughts: what you understand, the questions you have, and the ideas you want to explore further. Writing in your own words helps you work through the material and gives you something to return to as your understanding develops.

Thoughtful notes can also provide concrete examples of your independent learning when you approach research labs or apply to graduate school. They can show how you have explored ideas beyond your classes and help you explain your interests and the questions you would like to pursue.

## Exploring graduate courses

Ziyang encouraged members to explore the [U of T graduate CS timetable](https://web.cs.toronto.edu/graduate/timetable) and consider taking courses that match their interests and preparation. Do not assume that you must wait until fourth year to begin engaging with graduate-level material.

The discussion emphasized mathematical maturity: the ability to reason carefully, think logically, and work with proofs. Many theory topics do not require a long sequence of calculus courses. Basic linear algebra and probability can provide a useful starting point, with the background needed depending on the topic. Some theory courses emphasize mathematical maturity over a fixed list of prior courses; check the [course descriptions](https://web.cs.toronto.edu/graduate/course-descriptions) and discuss your preparation with the instructor.

**Enrolment clarification:** Taking a graduate course for credit requires approval through the [CS undergraduate enrolment process for 2026–2027](https://web.cs.toronto.edu/graduate/undergraduate-enrolment). The [Arts & Science calendar](https://artsci.calendar.utoronto.ca/course-enrolment) describes this permission as exceptional and expects students to have exhausted relevant undergraduate offerings. Check the course-specific preparation and permission requirements when planning to enrol.
