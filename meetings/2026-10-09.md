## AI and theoretical computer science

We discussed the opportunities and risks of using AI in theoretical computer science (TCS). AI can help us learn and make progress in research. We remain responsible for our own thinking, understanding, and the work we share.

### Opportunities for learning and research

Many areas of TCS are difficult to learn at first. AI can help students learn unfamiliar material and mathematical tools more quickly and apply them to research. Careful use of AI can help researchers get better results faster.

### Independent thinking and research credit

If we rely on AI too much, it can replace our own thinking and understanding.

Guiding AI through many conversations toward a research goal can create a false sense of ownership of the result. Those conversations alone do not justify taking research credit. We need to understand the technical details and be able to check correctness. We should be able to explain the reasoning ourselves and assess whether it is sound.

### Our no-AI-slop policy

Our team does not permit members to share AI-generated research material that they have not understood and checked. Fear that someone else will publish first does not justify publishing an unchecked, AI-generated paper.

We can keep AI-generated ideas and drafts private while we study them. Before we share or publish the resulting work, we must understand the techniques and check correctness. We must write and present the work based on our own understanding. A document that looks polished does not remove these requirements.

## Why take notes

Notes record your own thoughts: what you understand, your questions, and the ideas you want to explore further. Writing in your own words helps you work through the material and gives you a record to revisit as your understanding develops.

Thoughtful notes can provide concrete examples of independent learning when you approach research labs or apply to graduate school. They can show how you explored ideas beyond your classes and help you explain your interests and the questions you want to pursue.

## Exploring graduate courses

Ziyang encouraged members to explore the [U of T graduate CS timetable](https://web.cs.toronto.edu/graduate/timetable) and consider courses that match their interests and preparation. Do not assume that you must wait until fourth year to study graduate-level material.

The discussion emphasized mathematical maturity: the ability to reason carefully, think logically, and work with proofs. Many theory topics do not require a long sequence of calculus courses. The required background depends on the topic, but basic linear algebra and probability can provide a useful starting point.

Some theory courses emphasize mathematical maturity over a fixed list of prior courses. Check the [course descriptions](https://web.cs.toronto.edu/graduate/course-descriptions) and discuss your preparation with the instructor.

Taking a graduate course for credit requires approval through the [CS undergraduate enrolment process for 2026–2027](https://web.cs.toronto.edu/graduate/undergraduate-enrolment). The [Arts & Science calendar](https://artsci.calendar.utoronto.ca/course-enrolment) describes this permission as exceptional and expects students to have exhausted the relevant undergraduate course offerings. When you plan to enrol, check each course's preparation and permission requirements.
