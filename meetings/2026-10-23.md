## Notation for additive secret sharing

Work over a finite field $\mathbb{F}$. We write **$[x]$** to mean an additive secret sharing of $x$. For Alice and Bob, this consists of shares $x_A,x_B\in\mathbb{F}$ satisfying $x_A+x_B=x$: Alice holds $x_A$ and Bob holds $x_B$. The notation $[x]$ refers to the sharing held jointly by the parties; each party holds its own share.

## Local addition of shared values

Given $[x]$ and $[y]$, Alice computes $s_A=x_A+y_A$ and Bob computes $s_B=x_B+y_B$. These form an additive sharing of $x+y$, since

$$
s_A+s_B=(x_A+y_A)+(x_B+y_B)=x+y.
$$

We write this sharewise operation as $[x]+[y]=[x+y]$. Each party uses only the shares they already hold, so the computation requires **no communication**. The sum remains shared.

## Beaver triples

A **Beaver triple** consists of additive sharings

$$
([a],[b],[c]),\qquad c=ab,
$$

where $a,b\in\mathbb{F}$ are independent and uniform. Equivalently, we can write the triple as $([a],[b],[ab])$. Alice and Bob each hold their own share of each value.

The triple is prepared independently of the inputs, with fresh random additive shares, and used for one multiplication. The explanation assumes that Alice and Bob follow the protocol and already hold a correctly generated triple.

## Beaver multiplication

Suppose Alice and Bob hold $[x]$ and $[y]$. Using a Beaver triple $([a],[b],[c])$, their goal is to compute $[xy]$.

They first subtract their shares locally to compute

$$
[d]=[x]-[a],\qquad [e]=[y]-[b].
$$

Alice and Bob simultaneously send their shares of $d$ and $e$ to each other. Both can then reconstruct the public values $d=x-a$ and $e=y-b$.

Using these public values, they compute the output sharing locally:

$$
[z]=[c]+d[b]+e[a]+de.
$$

Multiplying a sharing by a public value means that each party multiplies its own share by that value. Adding the public term $de$ means that Alice adds $de$ to her share and Bob adds $0$ to his share, so the term is included exactly once.

The resulting sharing is $[z]=[xy]$, since

$$
z=c+db+ea+de=(a+d)(b+e)=xy.
$$

This uses **one simultaneous online communication round**, with the triple already available. Generating the triple is preprocessing and is excluded from this round count. The output remains shared; Alice and Bob do not reconstruct $xy$.

## Potential research directions: Condorcet sets

Ziyang introduced Condorcet sets and suggested two potential research directions.

In an election where each voter strictly ranks the candidates, a **Condorcet winning set** is a set $W$ such that, for every candidate $c$ outside $W$, a strict majority of voters prefer at least one member of $W$ to $c$. The preferred member of $W$ can differ from voter to voter. The **Condorcet dimension** of an election is the size of its smallest Condorcet winning set.

One proposed question is: **What is the smallest integer $k$ such that every election with strict voter rankings has a Condorcet winning set of size at most $k$?**

A second direction is to **develop nontrivial algorithms for finding small Condorcet winning sets** in a given election.

Ziyang pointed to [*Is Four Enough? Automated Reasoning Approaches and Dual Bounds for Condorcet Dimensions of Elections*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19851v1), by Itai Zilberstein, Ratip Emin Berker, George Li, and Ruben Martins (arXiv v1, April 21, 2026). Section 2.1 gives the definitions above; the paper is a starting point for exploring the size-bound question.
