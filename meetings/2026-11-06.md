# Secure multiparty computation and malicious security

**Date:** Friday, November 6, 2026

Ziyang introduced the general definition of secure multiparty computation (MPC), explained semi-honest and malicious adversaries, and presented the simulation-based definition of malicious security. We then discussed classical feasibility results and research on making MPC protocols efficient.

## What is secure multiparty computation?

MPC lets parties compute an agreed function of their private inputs. If party $P_i$ holds input $x_i$, the function specifies outputs

$$
(y_1,\ldots,y_n)=f(x_1,\ldots,x_n),
$$

where $P_i$ should receive $y_i$. The security goal is to emulate a trusted party that privately receives the inputs, computes the function, and returns the prescribed outputs. The outputs themselves may reveal information about the inputs; the protocol should protect information beyond what the specified ideal computation allows.

## Semi-honest and malicious adversaries

A **semi-honest adversary** controls parties that follow the protocol but try to learn additional information from their inputs, randomness, and received messages. Corrupted parties can combine this information.

A **malicious adversary** can make corrupted parties deviate arbitrarily from the protocol. For example, they may send incorrect messages, use different inputs, or stop participating.

**Malicious security does not force a party to supply a truthful input.** In the age-sum example, a party whose age is 20 can submit 40 instead. Even a trusted party cannot detect this lie from the submitted number alone. The security guarantee concerns computation on the submitted inputs; verifying facts about the outside world requires additional evidence.

## Defining malicious security

We compared two executions:

- In the **real execution**, the parties run the protocol, and the adversary controls the corrupted parties.
- In the **ideal execution**, a trusted party performs the specified computation. An ideal-world adversary, called a **simulator**, chooses the corrupted parties' inputs and uses only the information and actions allowed by the ideal computation.

The core requirement is: for every admissible real-world adversary, there exists an admissible simulator such that, for all allowed inputs and auxiliary information, the joint distributions of the adversary's output and the honest parties' outputs in the two executions are indistinguishable under the chosen security notion. The simulator's output takes the place of the adversary's output in the ideal execution.

The definition fixes the corruption model and the output-delivery rules. In particular, **security with abort** allows the adversary to prevent honest parties from receiving their outputs in the manner permitted by the ideal computation. Malicious security alone does not imply guaranteed output delivery.

## General feasibility and efficient protocols

Ziyang cited three classical results:

- **[Yao86]** Andrew C. Yao, [*How to Generate and Exchange Secrets*](https://luca-giuzzi.unibs.it/corsi/Support/papers-cryptography/04568207.pdf), FOCS 1986: foundational work on general two-party computation under cryptographic assumptions.
- **[GMW87]** Oded Goldreich, Silvio Micali, and Avi Wigderson, [*How to Play any Mental Game or a Completeness Theorem for Protocols with Honest Majority*](https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~oded/annot/node31.html), STOC 1987: general MPC under cryptographic assumptions, including malicious security with an honest majority.
- **[BGW88]** Michael Ben-Or, Shafi Goldwasser, and Avi Wigderson, [*Completeness Theorems for Non-Cryptographic Fault-Tolerant Distributed Computation*](https://www.math.ias.edu/~avi/PUBLICATIONS/MYPAPERS/GBW88/GBW88.pdf), STOC 1988: information-theoretic MPC over synchronous private authenticated channels, secure against semi-honest corruption of fewer than half the parties or malicious corruption of fewer than one third.

These results establish general feasibility under their respective assumptions. We ended by discussing a major direction in MPC research: **building efficient protocols**, including reducing computation, communication, and the number of communication rounds.
